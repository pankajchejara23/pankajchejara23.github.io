A software application planned for healthcare domain in EU (European Union) countries comes under the same EU regulation sets out for medicinal physical products. However, not every software used in healthcare falls under MDR.

In this post, we will develop a basic understanding around fundamentals of EU MDR. In particular, we will cover the following topics.

  • What is EU MDR?
  • Which software applications fall under its scope?
  • How they are classified and what does it entail to classified under those categories?
  • Who will provide certification over compliance?

What is EU MDR?

EU MDR is a legally binding regulation on medical devices (EU 2017/745) which must be fulfilled by each medical device manufacturer. The primary aim of this regulation is to ensure a smooth functioning of the market, and sets standard for quality and patients’ safety.

In simple terms, this regulation sets out rules each business, manufacturing products for medical purposes, must comply to minimize risks associated with the usage of the product in order to ensure patients’ safety.

Let’s imagine a scenario with a monitoring device for heart health (e.g., echocardiograph). Here, the output of the machine used by a health professional in decision-making on the diagnosis of a patient. Here, an errouneous results from the machine may lead the professional to a wrong judgement (e.g., everything seems fine) which can delays in treatment resulting in deterioration of the patient’s health.

EU MDR aim is to make businesses to be thoroughly and systematically in assessing the risks associated with their product’s usage, and planning mitigation strategies along with preparing the clinical evidence on ‘product does what it aims to do’. In that way, risk assessment and mitigation becomes a part of product development lifecycle not a afterthought after a serious incident.

What qualifies a software application for EU MDR?

A software application qualified as a medical device, is suject to MDR regulation. What makes a software medical device depends on the “intended use” of that software.

This “intended use” is legally determined based on how the software is marketed, advertised, and put in labels and IFU (Instructions for Use).

EU MDR has a specific section (Rule 11) which focuses on Software as a Medical Device. Guidelines from Medical Device Coordination Group (MDCG) state that a software that is intended for human beings for medical purpose of

  • diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction, prognosis, disease, treatment or alleviation of disease
  • diagnosis, monitoring, treatment, alleviation of, or compensation for, an injury or disability

There are four categories (I, IIa, IIb, III) from lower to higher risk. Each category has its own requirement to be fulfilled.

  • Category I: All softwares that do not fall in IIa, IIb and III.

  • Category IIa: Softwares intended with medical purpose of supporting a human beign in deciding for diagnosis or therapeutic. Software which are intended for monitoring vital physiological parameters such as heart rate falls under the scope of class IIa.

  • Category IIb: If the information from the software could lead to serious deterioration of a patinet’s heath or could impact surgical intervention then such software would go under class IIb. Moniotring software (vital physiological measurements) will fall under class IIb if variation in measurements could result in immediate danger to the patient

  • Category III: Softwares used for diagnosis or treatment in cases where incorrect information from the software could lead to critical incidents such as death.

How a software is qualified as MDSW and classified through risk-based assessment?

The first step is to determine whether the software application in question qualifies as MDSW (Medical Device Software). And, here the most crucial information is the software intended purpose. If the software is meant to be used for medical purposes then it is MDSW.

However, here are some exceptions to this rule. Softwares which are simply used for data storage, displaying, transferring or archival purposes are not qualified as medical devices.
